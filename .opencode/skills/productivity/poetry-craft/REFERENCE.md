# poetry-craft — Reference

The studio handbook. Red-flag census first (run it every pass), then the craft spine, then the exercise library. Principles paraphrase the lineage of Mary Oliver's *A Poetry Handbook* — sound, line, imagery, diction, revision, solitude — recast for an editor-agent.

## Red-flag census (run every pass)

- **Abstraction clusters** — emotion or concept stated where a thing could stand ("balancing act" family). Fix test: could a camera or a shovel find it?
- **Telling the feeling** ("to my surprise") — let the turn and the lineation create it.
- **Register breaks** — first name the poem's register in one phrase ("hymn-adjacent"), then flag words that crack it ("flabbergasted" in a hymn). A deliberate crack is style; an unowned one is a typo in costume.
- **Dangling participles / attachment fog** at line joints — walk the grammar: which noun owns each participle?
- **Stacked-hinge sentences** — count participial joints; more than two in one sentence → consider a stop or a dash as the shutter.
- **Dead idioms** — cut, or reanimate by making them literal ("cut me down to size", "flesh and blood").
- **Unearned marks** — trailing punctuation that stalls rather than opens. Run a dash census: each dash must do a distinct job (cut, descent, address, pivot, breath).
- **Unowned choices** — inconsistent capitalization, line-break habits broken without cause.
- **Soft generic closers** ("a future not yet known") — push one notch more concrete or stranger; physical first, mystery second ("knotted and unknown").

## The line

- Line-ends are prime real estate — end lines on words that deserve emphasis.
- Enjambment enacts suspension ("in the order / of things"); stanza breaks are breath-groups. Match the poem's own break-habit before inventing a new one.
- One-line stanzas are hinges and hammers; the lone flat line can carry a whole turn ("Then I returned to my concrete home of hurry.").
- Deceleration: a longer final line lets a stanza set down its weight; a falling unstressed close stoops the sentence.

## Sound

- Compressed doublets hum like litany ("knotted and unknown"): shared consonants, shadowed vowels.
- Idiom has its own music — reanimated idioms chime twice.
- Aloud test, always twice: once alone, once to a human. Where the breath stops is the poem's anatomy.

## Imagery

- Camera test: every line is a thing a camera finds, or a hinge between things.
- Descent-as-craft: g upward gaze made to drop (crown → burls → humus) can enact humility; track the poem's eyeline.
- Name vs describe: blunt specialist words ("burl") can be truer than soft description — use when the word sounds like the thing.

## Diction, register, voice

- Name the register at the top of each critique; audit violations against it.
- Passive voice is a stance, not a weakness — check the pattern it belongs to ("planted by", "cared for by" = receptivity as theology).
- A **register event** — first "you", sudden imperative, a solitary capitalized noun — costs something. Spend once, spend well.
- Borrowed gravity: words that carry scripture or myth in their marrow ("giant" = earthborn; "benediction") work without explanation.

## Facts and faith

- Anchored poems (biological, historical, scientific): the fact stays true *underneath*; the poem never explains it. Write the faith; let the fact be true underneath.
- The fact often answers to something already in the draft (the poem keeps "voting" for its own fact — watch for words like "birthed" pointing at burls).
- Exhibition poems can offload science to the wall card / artist statement.

## Punctuation as meaning

- Period trusts. Question mark kneels. Dash catches breath (the poem cut off where the speaker is). Ellipsis fades beyond words — earned ending or stall; decide.
- Comma pivots; colon delivers; a dash can be the shutter between gaze-levels.
- The mark must match the poem's system — five dashes in twenty-nine lines is a style only if each does distinct work.

## Revision protocol

- **Deletion test**: cut the weakest lines, read aloud; if nothing is lost, they're gone.
- **Trade, don't add**: when inserting new material, delete equal abstraction to keep stanza length honest.
- **24-hour dwell** before permanent media (ink, print, frame). Drafts are cheap; iron gall is forever.
- Between major passes, redraft by hand on scrap — bad-on-purpose beats cursor-polish.

## Exercise library (15–30 min each)

**Sound**
1. Rewrite one stanza three ways: maximum alliteration; monosyllables only; long vowels only. Read each aloud. Keep the best body, note what each forced.
2. Doublet hunt: find one plain pair in a draft ("not yet known") and compress it into a sounding doublet ("knotted and unknown"). Five minutes, one line.

**The line**
3. Take a prose paragraph; break it three ways; mark where emphasis moved with each break.
4. One-line hammer: reduce a stanza's whole second half to a single flat line. Does it hinge or sag?

**Imagery**
5. Camera pass: underline every word a camera can't find. Trade half of them for found objects.
6. Eyeline map: mark where the poem's gaze points each line (up/level/down). Redraft one stanza so the gaze enacts the meaning.

**Diction & register**
7. Name your draft's register in one phrase; list three words that break it; decide deliberate-or-cut for each.
8. Same stanza, two registers (vernacular / hymn). Compare what each costs.

**Facts & faith**
9. Choose one true fact about your subject. Write a stanza that never mentions it but cannot be false beside it.

**Punctuation**
10. Repunctuate one stanza three ways (periods only / dashes only / colon-delivery). Read aloud; note meaning shifts.

**Revision**
11. Delete 20% of a draft. Defend each survivor aloud in one sentence.

**Solitude & attention (the Oliver spine)**
12. Noticing walk: 20 minutes, notebook, no phone; five attended things per outing, one line each.
13. Hand-copy one master poem; then write one in-the-manner-of the same poet this month.
14. Lessons ledger: after each session log date, craft focus, what changed in the next poem. Review monthly.
